Collaboration · Product Design

Lomi: Genie

extending the countertop composter from food scraps to the nursery

Lomi: Genie

DS4 Studio · with Akshi Maiti

01 · The Opportunity

The kitchen got a machine. The nursery didn't.

Lomi launched in 2021 as a countertop electric composter, built to make food waste reduction simple and accessible at home. It positioned itself as a tech-forward answer to everyday sustainability — and it worked, for one category of household waste.

Food waste has tons of innovation. Baby waste has some. Pet waste has almost none.

Mapping where that innovation actually landed exposes the gap. Food waste has a mature product category. Baby waste has partial answers — biodegradable diapers, subscription pickup services — that still end in a landfill. Pet waste has almost nothing. Genie takes the system Lomi already built and points it at the category with the most volume and the least design attention.

02 · The Case

Why diapers and wipes

Disposable baby products make up a large share of landfill waste, and the numbers compound quickly. Roughly 27 billion disposable diapers are discarded each year — the equivalent of about 2.2 million cars packed full of waste. A single baby accounts for around 6,000 diapers and 13,140 wipes in the first year alone, before any toilet training begins.

None of it breaks down quickly. The mix of plastics, wood pulp, and absorbent polymers means a single-use product can take 450 to 500 years to decompose. Meanwhile the market for biodegradable diapers and wipes is projected to keep climbing through 2030 — demand for a better answer exists, but the hardware to handle it at home doesn't.

03 · The Audience

Who this is for

Genie is aimed at households already predisposed to bring a system like this indoors — people for whom the appliance has to earn its counter space on looks as much as function.

  • Urban & apartment residents
  • Gardeners & plant enthusiasts
  • Design & tech forward consumers

04 · The Concept

Pellets, not compost

The process runs from soiled diapers and wipes through pre-shredding and composting to a dry, uniform pellet. Stopping at compost was the obvious move; pushing one step further to pellets was the better one, for three reasons.

Easier integration. Pellets translate organic waste into a cleaner, more controlled, genuinely usable material — which makes circular practices far easier to fold into indoor life.

Hygiene and cleanliness. Baby waste is an already-sensitive category. A dry pellet output reads as hygienic and manageable in a way loose compost does not, which is what makes it acceptable in a home.

Expanding Lomi. Rather than ending at compost, the system standardizes its output. Pellets are easier to store, transport, and integrate into whatever comes next.

Positioned against the existing market — luxury through mass-market utility on one axis, sustainable impact on the other — the opening sits in the high-impact, high-end quadrant that nothing currently occupies.

05 · Brand Direction

Three pillars

Before any form work, the brand was pinned to three qualities that every subsequent decision had to answer to.

Integrated

Designed to fit seamlessly into daily routines, blending function, form, and environment into one cohesive home system.

Gentle

A soft, non-intrusive approach that makes waste processing feel approachable and safe inside everyday home environments.

Organic

Rooted in natural cycles — transforming waste in a way that feels intuitive, material-driven, and connected to ecological processes.

06 · Direction One

Quiet Care

The first direction leans hardest on Organic and Gentle. It's a stacked tower in soft blue and cream that reads as furniture rather than equipment: storage for soiled diapers and wipes up top with a self-cleaning throttle, a composting bucket that handles dehydration and pellet-forming in the middle, and pellet storage below holding roughly a week's output.

Back filter. A carbon filter that moves enough air to dehydrate the waste while blocking both odor and moisture.

Light indicator. A quiet band that signals when the composter is running, so it's obvious at a glance when it needs starting again.

Foot button. The lower cabinet opens and closes by foot — the one interaction most likely to happen with a baby already in your arms.

07 · Direction Two

Integrated Care

The second direction sits closer to Lomi's existing product language — a compact green-and-white bin with a flip lid, sized to live on a changing table rather than beside it. Internally it runs lid, filter, and hopper into a flat die and roller, with bucket, blade, motor, back filter, and discharge slope below.

The top section detaches for access to the pellet store and the refillable components, the filter included, so maintenance never means moving the whole unit. Of the two directions, this is the one that reads most naturally as a Lomi product.

08 · Following Concept

Lomi: Pet Bin

The same logic extends to the category with the least existing technology. In cities, dog waste pollutes waterways and adds harmful bacteria to rivers — the EPA classifies pet waste as a nonpoint source pollutant, putting it in the same bracket as oil or chemical runoff.

US dogs produce more than 10 million tons of waste annually, with around 6.5 million tons left uncollected or sent to landfill. It is also the most hazardous of the three streams: dog waste can carry parasites, E. coli, and roundworms, and cat waste can carry toxoplasmosis. The concept explores a wall-mounted unit with a transparent compartment, slider, and fill-level indicator.

09 · Bringing It To Market

A family, not a product

Genie and the Pet Bin were developed as one product family sharing a material language and a single processing logic, so the range reads as a system rather than a set of separate appliances.

The go-to-market strategy pairs that with a physical pop-up built around live interaction, an integrated app handling monitoring and cycle control, and a social program carrying the brand's quieter, more domestic tone.
